A Visual Introduction to Decision Trees

New York or San Francisco? A home has a price, a size and an elevation. Can those measurements tell us which city it is in?

Follow real housing data from a first visual rule to a decision tree. Then check whether the tree also works on homes it has never seen.

Start with elevation.

San Francisco is hilly. In these training data, every home above 73 m is in San Francisco.

That suggests a rule. But many San Francisco homes lie below that height too. One measurement does not perfectly separate the cities.

Add a second variable.

Now place the same homes by price per square foot as well as elevation.

Some low-lying New York homes are very expensive per square foot. Two variables reveal a pattern that one variable could not show.

Turn patterns into rules.

Try two illustrative rules: predict San Francisco above 73 m; below that height, predict New York when the price exceeds $1,776 per ft².

These boundaries divide the plot into regions. The remaining homes still need a rule. These thresholds are a starting intuition, not a fitted tree.

Look at all seven variables.

The file also records price, floor area, construction year, bedrooms and bathrooms. A scatterplot matrix compares every pair.

A decision tree searches for useful splits across these variables. We use only the training homes to choose those splits.

Find a useful split.

A histogram shows how many homes lie in each elevation interval. The colors show their cities.

Move the split below the chart. Homes on one side are predicted as New York; homes on the other as San Francisco.

Every split has errors.

Treat San Francisco as the positive class. An SF home predicted as NY is a false negative. An NY home predicted as SF is a false positive.

Changing the threshold changes both error counts. A split at 73 m avoids false positives in the training data, but misses many SF homes.

Let the data choose.

A tree prefers splits that make the two groups more homogeneous. Here we minimize their size-weighted entropy (log base 2). Zero means both groups are pure; this is equivalent to maximizing information gain.

For elevation, the best training split is 40 m. It is also the best first split across all seven variables. Even this split makes some errors.

Repeat within each branch.

Now repeat the search separately among the lower and higher homes. Each branch can choose a different variable and threshold.

This repeated procedure is called recursion. A terminal group is a leaf; its prediction is the city most common among its training homes.

Let the test homes pass through.

This tree has a fixed depth of 4. The 148 test homes now follow its rules. None of these observations was used to fit the tree.

Each home follows its own path to a leaf, then joins the group for its predicted city below. We compare the prediction with its actual city and count the correct classifications.

Grow the tree.

As you scroll, the tree grows from two levels to its full depth. More levels allow more detailed rules. You can also adjust the depth below the chart.

At full depth, it correctly classifies 100% of the training homes. That tells us how well it fits familiar observations; it does not establish how well it generalizes.

Follow one home.

Start at the top. Answer each question using the selected home’s measurements. Continue until you reach a leaf.

The leaf gives the prediction. Select a training or test home to inspect its full route and compare the prediction with its actual city.

Test on unseen homes.

Apply the fitted tree to the 148 held-out homes. None of them was used to choose a split.

At full depth, training accuracy is 100%, but test accuracy is 89.2%. Extra detail can fit peculiarities of the training sample: overfitting.

What did the tree learn?

A decision tree turns measurements into a sequence of questions. It chooses splits from training data and predicts a city at each leaf.

A perfect training score is not enough. Performance on unseen homes tells us more about whether the learned rules are useful.
